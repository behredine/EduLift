Student Guide — How a Power Transformer Works

Use the simulator above as you read. Click, rotate, zoom and pull the transformer apart — every part can be selected and inspected, so you can match what you read with what you see.

1 · What is a transformer?

A transformer is a machine that changes the voltage of an alternating current (AC) without any moving parts. Power plants generate electricity at high voltage, and transformers step it down in stages until it is safe to use in homes and factories. This model shows a distribution transformer that steps 115 kV down to 20 kV.

2 · How does it change voltage?

Inside the tank are two coils of wire — the windings — wrapped around a common iron core. When alternating current flows through one winding (the primary), it creates a constantly changing magnetic field in the core. That changing field induces a voltage in the other winding (the secondary). This effect is called electromagnetic induction.

This only works because the magnetic field is changing — which is exactly why transformers work with AC and not with battery (DC) power.

3 · Why 115 kV and 20 kV? (The turns ratio)

The voltage depends on the number of turns of wire in each coil:

Vprimary ÷ Vsecondary = Nprimary ÷ Nsecondary

If the secondary coil has fewer turns, the voltage is stepped down (and the current steps up). High voltage is used for transmission because it loses less energy over long distances.

4 · The windings and the core

Turn on Internals and X-ray to see them inside the tank. The iron core is laminated — built from thin sheets — to reduce energy lost to eddy currents. The windings are many turns of insulated copper or aluminium conductor: the LV windings sit closest to the core, with the HV windings wrapped outside them.

5 · Why oil and radiators?

Power transformers get hot, and heat destroys their insulation. The insulating oil does two jobs: it insulates the windings and carries heat away to the radiators, where air (and the cooling fans) removes it. The conservator is an expansion tank that makes room for the oil as it expands when hot, and the silica-gel breather keeps moisture out of the tank. Try raising the Load slider and watch the oil temperature gauge climb.

6 · How the output voltage is kept stable

The on-load tap changer (OLTC) can add or remove a few turns of a winding while the transformer is running — slightly changing the turns ratio — so the output voltage stays steady even when the load changes.

7 · Safety and the smaller details

The HV and LV bushings are porcelain insulators that let the high-voltage conductors pass safely through the metal tank without touching it. The ground / earth terminal connects the tank to earth so it can never become live. The oil drain valve lets oil be sampled or replaced, the nameplate lists the transformer's ratings, and the WTI gauge measures the hottest spot in the windings — not just the oil temperature.
